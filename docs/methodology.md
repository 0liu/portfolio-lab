# portlab Methodology Notes

This file carries the math derivations behind the implementation. The README carries conclusions and trade-offs.

## Data & point-in-time (PIT) rule

**Cache.** The parquet cache stores vendor bars as delivered (UTC, split+dividend adjusted, SIP feed) and is committed, so every result in this repo reproduces with zero credentials. Refresh clamps `end` to the last complete UTC day, so a partial intraday bar can never enter the cache.

**Calendar.** The trading calendar is the union of per-asset daily bars on naive New York dates. Ragged panel edges (an asset listed late or delisted) are tolerated, so no data means no position. However, an interior gap raises: a hole inside an asset's history is a data defect, not information.

**The PIT rule.** The decision and its execution both happen at the $t-1$ close, using only prices through that close; the weights then earn the return realized over the next session, which the panel labels row $t$:

$$w_t \in \mathcal{F}_{t-1}, \qquad w_t \text{ earns } r_t = P_t / P_{t-1} - 1,$$

where $\mathcal{F}_{t-1} = \sigma(P_1, \ldots, P_{t-1})$ is the information set generated by all closes through $t-1$, and $w_t \in \mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ says the weights are a function of that information alone.

Nothing about day $t$ — not its close, not its return — can reach the weights that hold through it.

The shifts are baked into the signal definitions themselves rather than deferred to a downstream convention, and the engine's covariance window ends at $t-1$ by construction.

## Signals

Three signals, one differential combine. All lookbacks are computed on PIT-shifted prices (the day-$t$ value sees closes through $t-1$).

- **Time-series momentum (tsmom)**
Reference: Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012), "Time Series Momentum", *JFE* 104(2).
For horizons $k \in \{63, 126, 252\}$ days,

$$z^{(k)}_{i,t} = \frac{r_{i,\,t-1,\,k}}{\hat\sigma_{i,t}\,\sqrt{k}}, \qquad
\text{tsmom}_{i,t} = \frac{1}{3} \sum_{k} z^{(k)}_{i,t},$$
where $r_{i,t-1,k}$ is the trailing $k$-day return ending at the $t-1$ close and $\hat\sigma_{i,t}$ is the asset's own EWMA daily volatility (halflife `signal_vol_halflife_days`, warm-up of two halflives, shifted one day). The scaling is per-asset time-series, *not* cross-sectional: tsmom retains an absolute return channel ($r_{i,t-1,k}$) and can be simultaneously positive (or negative) for every asset — the classic risk-on/risk-off tilt.

- **Cross-sectional momentum (xsmom)**
Reference: Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency", *Journal of Finance* 48(1).
The trailing 252-day return excluding the most recent 21 days — the "12-1" convention: twelve months of momentum, skipping the most recent one, because the latest month is contaminated by short-term reversal that runs against the momentum effect. That return is then mapped to a symmetric centered rank

$$\text{xsmom}_{i,t} = \frac{2\,(\text{rank}_i - 1)}{n - 1} - 1 \in [-1, 1],$$
where $\text{rank}_i$ is tie-averaged (worst=1) for non-NaN assets $i$, and $n$ is the number of non-NaN assets that day. The cross-sectional mean is exactly zero by construction, a pure relative-ranking channel without net-exposure content.

- **Short-term reversal (st_rev)**
The negative of the past 5-day return, cross-sectionally z-scored and clipped:

$$\text{st\_rev}_{i,t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\frac{-r_{i,\,t-1,\,5} - \operatorname{mean}_j(-r_{j,\,t-1,\,5})}
{\operatorname{std}_j(-r_{j,\,t-1,\,5})},\; -L,\; +L \right),$$

where $r_{i,t-1,5}$ is the trailing 5-day return ending at the $t-1$ close and the mean and standard deviation run across assets $j$ on day $t$. Fast mean-reversion, measured relative to the cross-section rather than to the asset's own history, another relative channel, mean-zero up to the clip.

- **Differential combine**

$$s_{i,t} = \tfrac{1}{3}\big(\operatorname{clip}(\text{tsmom}_{i,t}, -L, +L) + a \cdot \text{xsmom}_{i,t} + \text{st\_rev}_{i,t}\big),$$
per day, with no NaN-skipping — a missing component poisons the composite for that asset-day (conservative: no data, no position). The design is deliberately differential: tsmom carries the absolute level, the other two are mean-zero relative overlays, so the composite's net tilt comes from tsmom alone.

$L$ (`clip`) and $a$ (`xsmom_scale`) share the default 2.0 but are separate parameters: $L$ is a blowout guard on an unbounded score, $a$ is a scale-matching factor on a score already bounded to $[-1, 1]$. Tuning one must never move the other.

- **From score to expected return.** $\mu_{i,t} = s_{i,t} \cdot \hat\sigma_{i,t}$ maps the unitless score back through each asset's own volatility. Its absolute scale is arbitrary — see the MVO section for why that costs nothing.

## Covariance Estimation

### EWMA covariance

Ages the caller-supplied PIT window with exponentially decaying weights

$$w_s \propto 0.5^{\,(T-s)/h}, \qquad h = \texttt{ewma\_halflife\_days},$$

so an observation $h$ days older carries half the weight. With the weighted mean $\bar x = \sum_s w_s x_s / \sum_s w_s$ and demeaned rows $d_s = x_s - \bar x$,

$$\hat\Sigma^{\text{EWMA}} = \frac{\sum_s w_s\, d_s d_s^\top}{\text{denom}},
\qquad
\text{denom} = \Big(\sum_s w_s\Big) - \frac{\sum_s w_s^2}{\sum_s w_s},$$

the second term being pandas' reliability (`bias=False`) correction, which reduces to the familiar $T-1$ when all weights are equal. Validated against `DataFrame.ewm().cov()` — an independent recursive implementation must produce the same matrix.

### Ledoit-Wolf constant-correlation shrinkage

Reference: Ledoit & Wolf (2004), "Honey, I Shrunk the Sample Covariance Matrix", *Journal of Portfolio Management* 30(4). Implementation follows the paper's appendix. Symbols below match [`estimation.py`](../portlab/estimation.py).

Let $X$ be the $T \times N$ demeaned return matrix and

$$S = \frac{1}{T} X^\top X$$

the maximum-likelihood sample covariance (divided by $T$, per the paper, not the unbiased $T-1$). The estimator is the convex blend

$$\hat{\Sigma} = \delta F + (1 - \delta) S, \qquad \delta \in [0, 1].$$

#### Target $F$ (constant correlation)
Keep every sample variance; replace every pairwise correlation with the average correlation $\bar r$:

$$\bar r = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i < j} r_{ij}, \qquad
f_{ii} = s_{ii}, \quad f_{ij} = \bar r \sqrt{s_{ii}\, s_{jj}}.$$

In code `r_bar = (corr.sum() - N) / (N*(N-1))`: summing the full matrix counts the diagonal ($N$ ones) plus both triangles, so subtracting $N$ and dividing by $N(N-1)$ equals the paper's upper-triangle mean by symmetry.

#### Shrinkage intensity
With

$$\delta = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\kappa}{T},\, 0,\, 1\right), \qquad
\kappa = \frac{\hat\pi - \hat\rho}{\hat\gamma},$$

the three ingredients are:

- $\hat\pi$ — sum of asymptotic variances of the sample covariances,

$$\hat\pi = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \hat\pi_{ij}, \qquad \hat\pi_{ij} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t} \big(d_{ti} d_{tj} - s_{ij}\big)^2.$$
Expanding the square and using $\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t d_{ti} d_{tj} = s_{ij}$ collapses the matrix to

$$\hat\pi_{\text{mat}} = (D^{\circ 2})^\top (D^{\circ 2})/T - S \circ S$$
(with $D^{\circ 2}$ the elementwise square and $\circ$ the Hadamard product), summed over all $(i, j)$.

- $\hat\rho$ — asymptotic covariance between sample and target. Diagonal terms have $f_{ii} = s_{ii}$, contributing $\operatorname{tr}(\hat\pi_{\text{mat}})$; off-diagonal terms chain through the two $\sqrt{\cdot}$ factors of $f_{ij}$:

$$\hat\rho = \operatorname{tr}(\hat\pi_{\text{mat}})
+ \frac{\bar r}{2} \sum_{i \neq j}
\left( \sqrt{\tfrac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}}}\, \vartheta_{ii,ij}
+ \sqrt{\tfrac{s_{ii}}{s_{jj}}}\, \vartheta_{jj,ij} \right),
$$

$$\vartheta_{ii,ij} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t}
\big(d_{ti}^2 - s_{ii}\big)\big(d_{ti} d_{tj} - s_{ij}\big),
$$
vectorized as $\theta = (D^{\circ 2} \circ D)^\top D / T - \operatorname{diag}(S)\, S$; its transpose supplies $\vartheta_{jj,ij}$.

- $\hat\gamma$ — squared Frobenius distance between target and sample, i.e. how far the target is from the data:

$$\hat\gamma = \lVert F - S \rVert_F^2 = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} (f_{ij} - s_{ij})^2.$$

**Intuition**: shrink more when the sample is noisy (large $\hat\pi$) and the target fits (small $\hat\gamma$). Two asymptotic regimes as $T \to \infty$:

- **Target correctly specified** (true correlation constant): $S \to F$ as $T$ grows, so $\hat\gamma \to 0$ at rate $1/T$ and $\delta$ does *not* vanish — persistent shrinkage toward a true target is correct behavior.
- **Target misspecified**: $\hat\gamma \to$ a positive constant while $\hat\pi / T \to 0$, so $\delta \to 0$ — the data overrule a wrong target.

#### Validation strategy
sklearn's `LedoitWolf` shrinks toward scaled identity. It has a different target *and* intensity formula, so it is a benchmark, not a test oracle. Correctness rests on:
1. the sample covariance equals `sklearn.empirical_covariance` and `np.cov(ddof=0)` exactly;
2. the vectorized implementation equals a literal loop transcription of the paper (to $10^{-12}$);
3. properties: symmetry, PSD, diagonal preservation, and shrunk correlations exactly $\delta \bar r + (1-\delta) r_{ij}$;
4. on a known-$\Sigma$ DGP both our estimator and sklearn's beat the raw sample covariance.

## Portfolio Construction

### Risk contributions and the Euler decomposition

Portfolio volatility $\sigma(w) = \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}$ is homogeneous of degree one: scaling every weight by $c > 0$ scales the volatility by exactly $c$, i.e. $\sigma(cw) = c\,\sigma(w)$. Euler's theorem then guarantees that the first-order sensitivities reconstruct the whole:

$$\sigma(w) = \sum_i w_i \frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial w_i},$$

so the decomposition below is exact, not an approximation:

$$ \text{CCR}_i \equiv \text{RC}_i = w_i \cdot \text{MCR}_i = \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma(w)} = \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}}, \qquad \text{MCR}_i = \frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma(w)},$$

where $\text{MCR}_i$ is the marginal contribution to risk of asset $i$. Normalized contributions $\frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{(w^\top \Sigma w)}$ sum to one.

### ERC: Equal Risk Contribution

*References*:

- Maillard, Roncalli & Teiletche (2010), "The Properties of Equally Weighted Risk Contribution Portfolios", *JPM* 36(4).
- Roncalli, *Introduction to Risk Parity and Budgeting* (2013).

**Definition**: long-only, fully invested weights $w$ with all $\text{RC}_i$ equal. Its existence and uniqueness are proven in the paper. No closed form for general $\Sigma$ with $n > 2$. Solve by numerically minimizing the variance of the risk contributions,

$$f(w) = \sum_{i} \left( w_i (\Sigma w)_i - \frac{w^\top \Sigma w}{n} \right)^2
\qquad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_i w_i = 1, \; w \ge 0.$$

**Analytic gradient.** With $m = \Sigma w$ and $g = w \circ m - \tfrac{1}{n}(w^\top \Sigma w)$,

$$\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial w_j} = \delta_{ij} m_i + w_i \Sigma_{ij} - \frac{2}{n} m_j,$$

$$\nabla f = 2 \left[ g \circ m + \Sigma (g \circ w) - \frac{2}{n} m \sum_i g_i \right],$$

using $\Sigma$'s symmetry for the middle term. Implementation details:

- **Scale normalization.** ERC weights are invariant to $\Sigma \to c\Sigma$, so $\Sigma$ is scaled to $\operatorname{tr} = n$ internally. On raw daily covariances ($\sim 10^{-4}$) the objective sits around $10^{-8}$ and its improvements are smaller still, close enough to the solver's convergence tolerance that SLSQP can stop on a step that is numerically meaningless rather than on a genuine optimum. Rescaling puts $f$ at $O(1)$, where the convergence test measures real progress. The contract is re-verified on the *unscaled* $\Sigma$.
- **Two failure gates.** SLSQP's `success` flag is the solver's self-report. The returned weights are additionally verified against the definition,

  $$\max_i \left| \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{w^\top \Sigma w} - \frac{1}{n} \right| \le 10^{-4}.$$

It's an $L^\infty$ check and the contract is for-all, so the worst asset decides. Either gate failing raises an exception. The tolerance is set from measurement, not taste: across every rebalance on the real 19-asset panel the worst equal-contribution error is $5.3\times10^{-5}$ (late 2020, when the collinear duration and credit sleeves make $\Sigma$ worst-conditioned), so $10^{-4}$ accepts every genuine solution with roughly a factor of two to spare.

- **Analytic special cases pinned by tests.**
  - $n=2 \Rightarrow$ inverse-vol for any correlation;
  - Diagonal or constant-correlation $\Sigma \Rightarrow$ inverse-vol;
  - Identical assets $\Rightarrow$ equal weight.

**Volatility ordering** (MRT 2010): $\sigma(\text{min-var}) \le \sigma(\text{ERC}) \le \sigma(\text{EW})$.

The minimum-variance leg is obtained from the MVO optimizer with $\mu = 0$, which reduces its objective to pure variance minimization. That optimizer carries a per-asset weight cap (`position_cap`, 0.25), so it returns the *capped* minimum-variance portfolio rather than the unconstrained one. The inequality above is a statement about unconstrained min-var, so the test only holds when the cap is not binding, which is checked by confirming the ERC solution itself sits within the cap. If it does, both portfolios live in the same feasible set and the ordering applies.

### MVO family

Objective, solved with cvxpy + Clarabel:

$$\max_{w} \; \mu^\top w - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w
- \lambda \lVert w - w_{\text{prev}} \rVert_1.$$

- **Long-only (MVO)**: $\sum_i w_i = 1$, $0 \le w_i \le \text{cap}$.
- **Long-short (MVO-LS)**: $|w_i| \le \text{cap}$, $\sum_i |w_i| \le G$, $\text{net}_{\min} \le \sum_i w_i \le \text{net}_{\max}$, where $G$ is the gross-exposure cap (default 2.0).

$\mu$ is a directional proxy with arbitrary scale. Replacing $(\mu, \gamma)$ by $(c\mu, c\gamma)$ multiplies the objective by $c>0$ and leaves the argmax unchanged, so $\gamma$ alone sets position size. The $L_1$ penalty is the cost-awareness knob. At daily frequency it is not a refinement — without it the long-short book's trading costs exceed its gross returns and the strategy loses money outright (see the sweep below).

**What the lambda sweep shows** (2017-2026, 19 ETFs, 5 bps per side):

| optimizer | freq | net Sharpe, $\lambda = 0$ | net Sharpe, $\lambda = 3\times10^{-3}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| mvo | weekly | 0.701 | 0.766 |
| mvo | daily | 0.479 | 0.734 |
| mvo_ls | weekly | 0.260 | 0.474 |
| mvo_ls | daily | $-0.201$ | $+0.326$ |

Without the penalty, the daily long-short book trades 184.5x its own value per year and pays 9.23% annually in costs, enough to turn a positive gross strategy into a net loss. Turning the penalty on cuts turnover by 3.1x to 4.6x across the four legs, and cost drag falls in exact proportion, because cost is just turnover times the 5 bps rate. The daily books gain most: a cost-aware daily book (0.734) ends up beating a cost-unaware weekly one (0.701), so pricing costs into the objective is a better lever than simply trading less often.

**Vol-targeting overlay.** Volatility scales linearly with exposure, so to hit an annualized target $\sigma^\star$ from a book with daily vol $\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}$, scale by

$$s = \frac{\sigma^\star / \sqrt{252}}{\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}},
\qquad s \leftarrow \min\!\left(s, \frac{G}{\sum_i |w_i|}\right),$$

reusing the frozen gross-exposure cap ($\sum_i |w_i| \le G$, the same constraint the long-short optimizer enforces) as the ceiling, rather than introducing a second leverage limit. A fully-invested long-only book stops summing to one after scaling: the residual is cash ($s < 1$) or financing ($s > 1$), by design.


## Backtest engine

Daily walk-forward loop with a configurable rebalance schedule: any pandas offset alias works, and the weekly variant drives the cost-sensitivity study. `EngineConfig` and `CostConfig` are frozen dataclasses alongside the others.

### Timeline convention (the core correctness contract)

The engine holds the same PIT discipline as the signals: nothing about day $t$ reaches the weights that hold through it.

- **Signal and estimation as of $t-1$.** On a rebalance date, both $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are built from data through the $t-1$ close, guaranteed by the signal shifts and by feeding the estimator a window that ends at $t-1$.
- **Target executed at the $t-1$ close.** The optimizer's weights $w_t$ are traded at the last price the signal saw, and hold across the session that produces $r_t = P_t/P_{t-1} - 1$.
- **Costs charged on execution.** Turnover against the drifted book is deducted at the moment of the trade, before the new position earns anything.

This ordering is what the tripwire tests lock: perturbing the day-$t$ close must leave every weight decided at or before $t-1$ bit-identical.

### Weight drift between rebalances

Between rebalances weights are not constant — they drift with realized returns. Write $\tilde w_t$ for the book that *arrives* at date $t$, after the previous target has been held through a session and before any new trade:

$$\tilde w_{t+1,\,i} = \frac{w_{t,i}\,(1 + r_{t,i})}{1 + r_t^{\text{net}}},\quad r_t^{\text{net}}=\sum_j w_{t,j}r_{t,j}-\text{cost}_t.$$
The denominator is the portfolio's realized growth net of cost. Cost is paid out of cash, so it lowers NAV and every surviving position's share is measured against that smaller base. With zero cost the denominator collapses to $\sum_j w_{t,j}\,(1 + r_{t,j})$, the familiar fully-invested form. Turnover at date $t$ is measured against $\tilde w_t$, not against the previous target: the book has moved on its own since that target was set, and only the remaining gap is actually traded.

### No-trade band

A rebalance only moves an asset if its target deviates from the drifted weight by more than a band $b$:

$$w_{t,i} =
\begin{cases}
w_{t,i}^{\text{opt}} & \text{if } |w_{t,i}^{\text{opt}} - \tilde w_{t,i}| > b, \\
\tilde w_{t,i} & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}$$

The band suppresses churn from tiny target changes; the band and the $\lambda$ penalty are the two cost-control levers, compared in the exhibits.

### Cost model

Proportional cost of $c$ per side (default 5 bps) on traded notional:

$$\text{cost}_t = c \sum_i \big| w_{t,i} - \tilde w_{t,i} \big|,$$

subtracted from the period return. Net portfolio return for row $t$:

$$r^{\text{net}}_{t} = \sum_i w_{t,i}\, r_{t,i} \; - \; \text{cost}_t.$$

**Everything is per unit of NAV.** Weights are fractions of portfolio value ($\sum_i w_i = 1$ long-only), so turnover $\sum_i |w_{t,i} - \tilde w_{t,i}|$ is the *fraction* of NAV traded, cost is a fraction of NAV, and gross/net returns are fractions of NAV — all dimensionless and directly additive ($r^{\text{net}} = \text{gross} - \text{cost}$ requires the same units on both sides). Absolute traded notional would be turnover $\times$ AUM, but the backtest is unit-normalized: with a linear per-side cost the Sharpe, CAGR and cost drag are all ratios, invariant to AUM, so account size never enters. Absolute quantities would only matter under size-dependent costs (market impact, minimum tickets, lot constraints), which this linear model excludes — a limitation to record, not a bug.

Turnover is measured against the *drifted* book $\tilde w_t$ and, when a no-trade band is on, against the *post-band* held weights (only assets that actually trade contribute), so cost equals real turnover $\times$ rate exactly.

### Accounting identities

- **Weight conservation.** Long-only books satisfy $\sum_i w_{t,i} = 1$ at every rebalance. Between rebalances the drifted sum obeys $\sum_i w_{(t+1),i}=(\sum_i w_{t,i} + \sum_i w_{t,i}r_{t,i}) / (1 + r_t^{\text{net}})$, which is exactly one only when costs are zero: paying a cost shrinks the NAV denominator, so a fully-invested book drifts to slightly above one until the next rebalance trims it back.
- **Cost is turnover $\times$ rate.** A hand-computed two-asset, two-date case pins $\text{cost}_t$ and $r^{\text{net}}_t$ to closed-form values.
- **Zero turnover $\Rightarrow$ zero cost**, and $\lambda \to \infty$ / band $\to \infty$ both collapse turnover to zero — the same monotonicity the optimizer contracts assert, now end-to-end through the engine.

## Attribution

Reporting conventions: 252 trading days per year, zero risk-free rate, one-way turnover as the engine measures it. With $T$ daily net returns $r_t$ and years $= T/252$:

$$\text{CAGR} = \Big(\prod_t (1+r_t)\Big)^{1/\text{years}} - 1, \qquad
\text{vol} = \operatorname{std}(r)\sqrt{252}, \qquad
\text{Sharpe} = \frac{\bar r}{\operatorname{std}(r)}\sqrt{252}.$$

Sortino replaces the denominator with the downside deviation about a zero target, $\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t \min(r_t, 0)^2}$; Calmar is CAGR over $|\text{MaxDD}|$; MaxDD (maximum drawdown) is the most negative value of $\text{equity}/\operatorname{cummax}(\text{equity}) - 1$; cost drag is the annualized sum of charged costs, in return units. Sharpe and Sortino guard against a zero denominator: a constant return series has no volatility and no defined ratio, so the statistic is NaN rather than an arbitrarily large number produced by floating-point noise.

**Market beta.** Against a benchmark return series $r_b$ (SPY buy-and-hold over the same window), measured on the overlapping dates:

$$\beta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(r, r_b)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_b)}.$$

Beta is what makes raw equity curves interpretable. Over a single equity bull decade, terminal wealth ranks by market exposure rather than by construction quality, and volatility is not a substitute for beta — the same 10% volatility can be entirely market risk or none of it. The core statistics stay benchmark-free so a comparison table is always computable; beta is an opt-in column.

**Risk decomposition.** Following the Euler identity derived in the Portfolio Construction section,

$$\text{MCR}_i = \frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma(w)}, \qquad
\text{CCR}_i = w_i \cdot \text{MCR}_i, \qquad
\sum_i \text{CCR}_i = \sigma(w),$$

with percentage contributions $\text{CCR}_i / \sigma(w)$ summing to one. These are reported per optimizer, both per asset and grouped by asset class. The decomposition is exact, so the percentage shares are a genuine budget: a negative share means the asset's marginal effect is to *reduce* portfolio volatility, which happens when its covariance with the rest of the book is negative enough to outweigh its own variance.

**Frontier shortfall.** The in-sample constrained frontier is traced by solving the long-only capped MVO over a grid of risk-aversion values $\gamma$, using full-sample $\mu$ and $\Sigma$. It is not tradable — it sees the entire sample — and serves only as a yardstick. For each realized book, the shortfall is the vertical distance to that curve at the book's own realized volatility:

$$\text{shortfall} = \hat{R}(\sigma_{\text{realized}}) - R_{\text{realized}},$$

where $\hat{R}$ linearly interpolates the frontier's annualized return between grid points. Both sides use annualized *arithmetic* returns ($\bar r \times 252$) to match the frontier's own axis, which is why these differ slightly from the geometric CAGR reported elsewhere. When a book's realized volatility falls outside the frontier's span the shortfall is NaN rather than an extrapolated number: a capped, fully-invested, long-only frontier cannot reach the risk level a leveraged long-short book attains, so there is no comparable point to measure against.
